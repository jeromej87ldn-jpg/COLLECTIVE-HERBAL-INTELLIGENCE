Getting Started
Everything you need to go from herb to preparation
A practical kit list for making your own tinctures and tea bags at home, plus what to have in place if you ever want to take it further and prepare herbal products for other people.
01 Making Tinctures at Home
A tincture is a herb steeped in alcohol (or vinegar/glycerin for alcohol-free versions) to draw out and preserve its active compounds. This is the core kit — most of it doubles up as normal kitchen equipment.
🫙Glass Jar with Lid
Wide-mouth glass holds the herb-and-menstruum mix while it steeps. Glass won't react with alcohol the way some plastics can, and a tight lid keeps air and evaporation out.
🔪Sharp Knife
For chopping roots, bark and leaves down small. A sharper blade gives cleaner cuts and more exposed surface area, which means a stronger, faster extraction.
🪵Cutting Board
Keep one board for herb prep only if you can — it avoids cross-flavours and keeps things sanitary between batches.
🧺Fine Mesh Strainer
Used once the tincture has finished steeping, to separate the liquid from the plant matter. A first pass through a strainer catches the bulk of the material.
🧻Cheesecloth or Muslin
A second, finer filter after the strainer. Lining the strainer with cheesecloth (or straining through it separately) removes the fine sediment for a clear, sippable tincture.
💧Glass Dropper Bottles
For storing and dosing the finished tincture. Amber or cobalt glass blocks light, which helps the tincture keep its potency for longer.
🧪Small Funnel
Makes it far easier to pour the strained tincture into narrow dropper bottles without losing half of it down the side.
🏷️Labels and Marker
Every jar and bottle should be labelled with the herb, the liquid used, the date it was started, and the date it's due to be strained.
02 Making Your Own Tea Bags
If tea is your preferred way of taking herbs, filling your own bags lets you control exactly what's in the blend and in what ratio.
🍵Empty Tea Filter Bags
Unbleached paper filter bags — either drawstring or heat-seal style — sized for a single cup or a full pot, depending on how you drink.
🥄Measuring Spoons
Keeps every bag consistent, so you know roughly how much of each herb you're taking cup to cup instead of guessing by eye.
♨️Small Iron or Straightener
A quick way to heat-seal filter bags shut on low heat if you're using the heat-seal style rather than drawstring bags.
🧵Natural Cotton String
For tying drawstring bags closed, or attaching a tag if you're gifting or batching several blends at once.
✂️Scissors
For trimming string, opening bags and tidying loose leaf that's too coarse to fold neatly.
🥣Small Bowl
For mixing multiple herbs into one blend evenly before it goes into the bag.
📎Stapler
A fast way to close a folded filter bag if you don't have a heat-sealer to hand — staple just above the herb line.
03 Capsules and Capsule Making
Capsules mask taste entirely and give a precise, repeatable dose — useful for bitter or strong-tasting herbs. The kit is small, but getting the right capsule size for your herb matters more here than with tinctures or tea.
💊Empty Capsules
Two-piece shells you fill with dried, finely powdered herb. Gelatin capsules are cheaper and dissolve slightly faster; vegetarian (HPMC/pullulan) capsules suit vegans and hold up better in humid conditions.
🧰Capsule Filling Machine
A manual tray-and-pin board that holds a batch of empty capsule bottoms upright so you can load powder into all of them at once. Home machines typically come in 24, 50, 100 or 200-hole trays — the larger the tray, the more capsules per batch.
🪪Tamping Tool
Presses the powder down evenly into each capsule half after filling, so doses stay consistent from one capsule to the next instead of some ending up half-empty.
🃏Powder Tray or Bench Scraper
A flat card or scraper for spreading herb powder across the loaded tray, so it settles into every hole evenly before you press the two capsule halves together.
⚖️Small Digital Scale
Capsule size tells you the available space, not the dose — every herb powder packs down differently. Weigh a handful of filled capsules the first time you use a new herb so you know your real per-capsule dose.
Capsule sizes run from 000 (largest) down to 5 (smallest). Your capsule machine, and the shells you buy for it, need to match the same size.

04 Safety and Next Steps
A few habits that make the difference between a clean, effective preparation and one that's wasted or unsafe.
✓Sanitize everything first. Jars, bottles, tools and boards — wash and dry thoroughly before they touch any herb. Moisture left behind is the most common cause of mould in a tincture or jar.
✓Chop or bruise herbs finely. The smaller the pieces, the more surface area is exposed to the liquid, and the stronger and faster your extraction will be.
✓Label the moment you start. Herb, liquid, start date, strain-by date. It's easy to forget which jar is which after a few weeks on the shelf.
✓Store cool, dark, and out of direct sun. Give tinctures a shake most days while they steep to keep the extraction even.
✓Check before you use it. If a tincture or dried herb smells off, looks cloudy in an unexpected way, or shows any mould, discard it rather than risk it.
✓Start small. Make a small batch the first time you work with a herb or method that's new to you, so a mistake costs you little.

05 Taking It Further: Starting as a Business
If home batches turn into something you want to sell, the equipment above is still the foundation — but a few extra habits become non-negotiable.
⚠️ This is general orientation, not legal, regulatory or financial advice. Rules for selling herbal products — labeling, licensing, cottage food laws, supplement regulations — vary significantly by country and region. Check with a local business advisor or the relevant regulatory body before selling anything.
◆Source consistently and traceably. Buy from identified, reputable suppliers and keep a record of where every ingredient came from and when.
◆Label rigorously. Full ingredient list, net weight, date made, and any allergen warnings — beyond the basic herb-and-date label that's fine for personal use.
◆Track by batch. Give every batch a lot number so it can be traced back to its ingredients and process if a question about it ever comes up.
◆Know your shelf life. Test and document how long each preparation stays potent and safe, and reflect that honestly on the label.
◆Check local regulations early. Cottage food laws, herbal supplement labeling requirements, and business licensing differ by location — confirm what applies to you before you sell your first bottle.
◆Get product liability insurance. It protects both you and the people using what you make.
